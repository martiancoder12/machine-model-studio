# Book I — C: The Machine Model

**Level range:** L0 → L3
**Tier:** Foundations
**Method:** French-with-Ash, re-pointed at systems programming
**Roadmap:** v2 — *OT as vehicle, PQC as destination*

This is the first of three books generated by one syllabus. Book I takes you from
nothing to L3 — the point where you can read unfamiliar C and catch its undefined
behaviour, by eye and by tool. Book II re-implements this book's artifacts under
compiler-enforced discipline (L3 → L4). Book III scores everything against an
external rubric — fuzzers, sanitizers, adversarial review (L4 → L5). Every seam this
book opens, Book II answers and Book III weaponizes and defends. Nothing you build
here is throwaway.

## The Method: From French-with-Ash to Systems Programming

You built a TEF/TCF exam-prep academy whose syllabus generated three books —
*Foundations*, *Consolidation*, *The Exam Sprint*. The academy worked because it
treated language acquisition as an engineering problem: define the target level,
sequence production before grammar, re-expose material on a schedule, gate
progression on checkpoint exams, and make learners explain publicly. This curriculum
is that method, re-pointed at systems programming. The six principles carry over
unchanged in spirit — what changes is the domain, and the way each principle lands
in C is worth stating precisely, because the whole book's structure is these six
principles made mechanical.

**1. Syllabus-first.** One syllabus generates three books. In the academy, the
syllabus was the contract: the books were its development, not its replacement, and
no chapter was allowed to improvise its own scope. The same holds here. This book
develops an approved syllabus — every cold open, seam, lab, build task, and defer
in it was decided before a line of prose was written. When a chapter tells you a
topic is deferred, that is the syllabus keeping its concept budget, not an author
running out of space. Trust the sequencing; it is the product.

**2. Three-tier progression.** *Foundations → Consolidation → Exam Sprint* maps to
*Machine Model → Discipline by Construction → The Adversarial Seam*. The mapping is
not cosmetic. In French, Foundations taught you the sound system and the high-frequency
structures; Consolidation made you re-encounter them under load until they were
automatic; the Exam Sprint scored you against the real exam's rubric. Here, Book I
teaches the machine — storage, addresses, layout, lifetime. Book II re-encounters
the same artifacts under Rust's discipline, so the rules you enforced by hand become
rules a compiler enforces for you — that is consolidation, deliberate and by name.
Book III is the exam sprint: it scores your work against an external rubric —
fuzzers, sanitizers, adversarial review — the way the Sprint scored you against the
real TEF/TCF. A parser that merely *works* fails Book III the way a sentence that
merely communicates fails the exam's production orale rubric.

**3. Production before comprehension.** Every module in this book opens with a cold
open: you write running — or deliberately failing — code *before* anyone explains
it. You will overflow a buffer before you know what a stack frame is. You will
produce a use-after-free with your own hands before the term exists for you. This is
speaking before the grammar lesson, and it is not a gimmick. The cold open gives the
exposition something to attach to: when the explanation arrives, it lands as the
answer to a confusion you personally produced, not as trivia. The rule is absolute —
never read ahead to "understand first." Run the code, watch it do the strange thing,
*then* let the chapter explain.

**4. Spaced re-exposure.** In the academy, vocabulary returned on a schedule whether
you felt ready or not. Here, re-exposure has two mechanisms. The small one: every
chapter ends with a **Reps** section — three to five short drills to run before the
next module: re-implement something small from memory, read a piece of code,
predict-then-run. They are unglamorous and they are where retention actually happens;
do them. The large one: Book II deliberately re-implements Book I artifacts under a
new discipline. The growable buffer you build by hand in I.4 returns in Book II.2,
re-built with compiler-checked ownership. The overflow you commit in I.5 returns in
Book II.1, where the compiler refuses it. That is the consolidation-tier move: the
same skill, re-encountered at the exact moment forgetting would otherwise begin.

**5. Level gates.** L3, L4, L5 are the NCLC-checkpoint analogue. You do not advance
a book until the gate artifact passes — not "you read the chapters," not "you
understood the prose," but a concrete artifact meeting published scoring criteria.
Book I's gate is stated in full at the end of this front matter: a library-plus-test-harness
that builds clean under sanitizers, a capstone parser you read, instrumented, and
extended, and the underlying skill performed unaided on C you have never seen. Gates
exist because understanding is easy to overestimate and artifacts are not.

**6. Feynman publishing.** Selected build tasks produce Laboratoires Structure blog
artifacts. Explaining publicly is part of the assessment, exactly as it was in the
academy: if you cannot teach it, you have not finished learning it. I.6's hand-decoded
wire header feeds the "Reading Modbus traffic in Wireshark" post; I.8's capstone
generates "What a PLC actually does." These are not optional garnish — they are the
evidence file you will point WT1 applications at in Fall 2027, and the act of writing
them is the final rep on each module's material.

## Book Anchor

C is the native language of the thing your OT lane secures. PLCs, RTUs, and IEDs run
C-family firmware, and they run it for twenty to thirty years — written before modern
tooling, patched under outage constraints, and audited, when audited at all, by people
who can read C fluently. That is why this book exists. Every concrete step on Roadmap
v2 presumes the language: the home lab, because OpenPLC is C and you cannot instrument
what you cannot read; SOEN 228's PLC/RTU-internals mapping, because the internals are
C; *Countdown to Zero Day* and *Sandworm*, which read as practitioner texts instead of
thrillers only once the bug classes doing the work are ones you have committed yourself;
and every WT1 industrial-firmware conversation you will have in Fall 2027, where "I
can read your codebase" is the sentence that separates a candidate from an applicant.
The L3 gate at the end of this book — reading unfamiliar C and catching undefined
behaviour — is not an academic milestone. It is the PLC-codebase audit skill, in
miniature, under your control.

## How to Use This Book

Every chapter is built to the same contract, in this order:

- **Cold Open** — full, runnable code and exact shell commands. You run it first,
  before any explanation, and watch it do something you did not expect. Production
  before comprehension.
- **Exposition** — two to four titled sections, one per concept area, developing the
  mental model the cold open just made you want. Small compiled examples throughout.
- **The Seam** — the attack surface or bug class the module exposes: buffer overflow,
  use-after-free, integer wraparound, type confusion. Every seam opened here is
  answered in Book II and weaponized-then-defended in Book III.
- **Lab** — a guided, numbered exercise: every command, every file, expected
  observations, closing on one sentence stating the rule.
- **Build Task** — the scored artifact for the module, with published scoring
  criteria. No reference solution is provided; the artifact is yours to produce and
  to defend.
- **Why This Matters for Your Roadmap** — the wire to Roadmap v2: work terms,
  courses, research problems, the blog. Substantive where a real hook exists; brief
  and candid where the connection is foundational.
- **Reps** — three to five short drills to run before the next module. Spaced
  re-exposure, made explicit. Do them.
- **Deferred** — scope deliberately parked, with the module where it lands.

Standing rules, in force from page one:

- **C11 everywhere.** Every listing compiles with `cc -std=c11 -Wall -Wextra`.
  Correct code compiles warning-free; when a listing intentionally provokes a
  warning, error, or undefined behaviour, the text says so and shows the real
  diagnostic.
- **Real output only.** Quoted output is output actually observed on a real machine.
  Where behaviour is machine-dependent — addresses, `sizeof` results, optimizer
  decisions — the text flags it as observed *on this machine* and tells you what may
  differ on yours.
- **Clean under sanitizers, from I.7 onward.** Once the instrumentation toolchain
  arrives in I.7, "compiles and runs" stops being sufficient: build artifacts must
  run clean under ASan/UBSan, and that requirement never lifts through Book III.

One procedural rule overrides all others: **never read the exposition before running
the cold open.** The confusion is the curriculum.

## Module Map

- **I.1 · Hello, machine** — the compilation pipeline and the shape of a C program.
- **I.2 · Values and the shape of memory** — fixed-width integers, two's complement, and overflow as a bug primitive.
- **I.3 · Pointers and addresses** *(make-or-break)* — the address model of the machine; everything later stands on this.
- **I.4 · The heap and manual memory** — malloc/free, ownership by hand, and the growable buffer.
- **I.5 · Arrays, strings, buffers** — decay, NUL termination, and the deliberate overflow.
- **I.6 · Structs and composing data** — layout, padding, unions, and binary wire formats.
- **I.7 · Multi-file programs and the real toolchain** *(L3 gate)* — headers, make, gdb, and the sanitizers.
- **I.8 · Undefined behaviour and reading real C** *(capstone)* — the UB catalogue and the parser you will be fuzzing in Book III.

## Gate Statement

You advance to Book II when all three hold:

1. The I.7 L3 gate build — the small library plus test harness — passes its scoring
   criteria, clean under ASan/UBSan.
2. The I.8 capstone — **the I.8 parser** — is read, instrumented, and extended per
   its criteria.
3. You can do the underlying skill unaided: given a C file you have never seen,
   produce an accurate map of it and catch its undefined behaviour, by eye and by
   tool.

That is L3. Book II assumes it.
